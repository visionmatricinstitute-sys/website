## Video script (60–90 seconds, talking-head or slides)

**Hook (0:00–0:10)**
"Walk into almost any data center and the racks all follow the same pattern: fronts facing fronts, backs facing backs. That's not random — it's one of the cheapest ways to cut a facility's cooling bill."

**Body (0:10–1:10)**
"Every server pulls in cool air at the front and blows out hot air at the back — usually 15 to 20°C hotter. Line racks up randomly, and that hot exhaust mixes straight back into the cold supply before it ever reaches the cooling units.

Hot aisle/cold aisle layout stops that: rack fronts face each other across a cold aisle, rack backs face each other across a hot aisle. No rack front ever faces another rack's back.

Layout alone helps. Containment — actually sealing that aisle with doors and roof panels — stops it almost completely. And it eliminates two specific failure modes: recirculation, where hot exhaust leaks back into an intake and causes real hot spots, and bypass, where cold air escapes before it ever cools anything.

Here's the part people skip: even a perfectly contained aisle leaks through empty, unused slots in a rack. That's what blanking panels are for — and it's one of the most common things commissioning teams actually find missing."

**Close / CTA (1:10–1:25)**
"This isn't just an HVAC decision — it drives cable tray routing, PDU placement, and how much spare capacity a row can actually support. We cover data hall layout like this as part of our Electrical Design, Data Center Specialist program. Link in bio / description."

## LinkedIn post draft

---
Every data center layout looks the same for a reason: rows facing each other, then rows facing away. That's hot aisle / cold aisle containment, and it's one of the cheapest levers in the whole facility.

The problem it solves: servers exhale what they inhale. No containment, and hot exhaust mixes straight back into cold supply air before it reaches the cooling units.

Two specific failures containment eliminates:
→ Recirculation — hot exhaust leaking back into an intake, causing real hot spots
→ Bypass — cold air escaping before it ever cools anything

Both directly inflate PUE. And the detail that's easy to skip: an empty, unblanked rack-unit slot is a leak point even inside a properly contained aisle — which is exactly why blanking panels show up so often on commissioning punch lists.
